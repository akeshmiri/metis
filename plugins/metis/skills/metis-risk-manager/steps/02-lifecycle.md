# 2 · Running the lifecycle without a specialist

When the request does not fall cleanly into one specialist's territory, run the
loop here. The order matters and skipping a step is the common failure.

## Identify → analyse → respond → monitor

**Identify first, rate second, and keep them separate.** Rating during
elicitation suppresses candidates: as soon as a number is attached, discussion
becomes about the number. Collect, then rate.

**Rate qualitatively before quantitatively.** A 5×5 ranking costs minutes and
orders the whole register. EMV and PERT cost real effort and need inputs that
often do not exist. Quantify the top band, not everything — quantifying a Low
risk is how a risk process becomes theatre.

**Respond only after ranking**, because the strategy depends on where the risk
sits. Everything gets a response is not a plan; it is a way of spending the
mitigation budget on the wrong risks.

**Monitor is not a phase at the end.** A risk with no indicator and no review
date is a risk nobody will look at again until it happens.

## Where Métis contributes

Métis can observe some risks and cannot observe most of them:

- **It can see** behaviour a change leaves unasserted (`risk_candidates`), and
  figures it could not measure (`coverage_report`'s `unmeasured`).
- **It cannot see** a supplier who is about to miss a date, a sponsor who is
  leaving, a regulation changing, or an architecture that will not scale.

So model-derived candidates are a **supplement to** identification, never a
substitute. A register built only from `risk_candidates` covers one category out
of ten and will look reassuringly full while doing it.

## Closing the loop

Feed what happened back: a risk that occurred should be checked against the
probability it was given. A process that never compares its forecasts to
outcomes cannot improve, and its numbers stay unfalsifiable.
